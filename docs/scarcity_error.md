# The Scarcity Error: Why Post-Scarcity Economics Is Just Correct Game Theory

*A framework for understanding why billionaires are running an irrational strategy, why artificial scarcity is both unethical and bad game theory, and why the Federation was always the correct answer.*

*By Danielle C. & Vesper Luna | CC-0*

## Introduction: The Assumption Nobody Examined

Classical economics is built on a foundation so foundational it's rarely stated explicitly: **resources are scarce.** There isn't enough for everyone. The pie is fixed. Someone wins, someone loses. From this assumption, an entire architecture of behavior follows logically: compete for resources, accumulate against future shortage, defend what you have, extract maximum value before someone else does.

For most of human history, this assumption was accurate. Food could run out. Land was finite. Energy was limited by what you could harvest from the sun, the wind, the bodies of animals, and the muscles of humans. Under genuine scarcity, competition is the rational strategy. The economics of hunter-gatherer societies, agricultural civilizations, and early industrial economies was not irrationality — it was adaptive behavior under real constraints.

But the assumption has not been re-examined with the same rigor as the systems built on top of it.

This is a framework for doing that examination. The finding is straightforward: **the scarcity assumption no longer holds for most of what modern economies treat as scarce.** And when the assumption fails, the strategies built on it fail with it — not morally, but mathematically. Post-scarcity economics isn't idealism. It's just correct game theory applied to current conditions.

## Part I: What Game Theory Actually Says

Game theory is the mathematics of strategic decision-making — how rational actors choose when their outcomes depend on what others choose.

**The ELI5:** Imagine you and a friend both reach for the last piece of pizza at the same time. If there's only one slice, you're competing: one of you gets it, one doesn't. If someone orders another pizza, you're not competing anymore — you're both waiting. The strategy changes completely depending on whether the pie is fixed.

The classic game theory scenario is the **Prisoner's Dilemma**: two players can each choose to cooperate or defect. If both cooperate, both do moderately well. If both defect, both do poorly. If one cooperates and one defects, the defector does very well and the cooperator does very badly.

In a single interaction with a stranger you'll never see again, defection is the mathematically dominant strategy. This is the foundation of the competitive model: in a world of one-shot interactions over fixed resources, defection — taking more than your share, undercutting the other party, extracting maximum value — is rational.

But the game changes dramatically under two conditions:

**Condition 1: Iterated play.** When you interact with the same people repeatedly over time, cooperation becomes the dominant strategy. The classic tit-for-tat analysis shows that in repeated games with indefinite horizons, players who cooperate conditionally — rewarding cooperation, punishing defection — consistently outperform pure defectors. The defector gets one good round and then faces retaliation for the rest of the game. Modern economies are iterated games.

**Condition 2: Expandable resources.** When the pie isn't fixed — when cooperation can grow the total available — defection stops making sense even in single interactions. If I take your sandwich, I have one sandwich. If we cooperate on building a food system, we both have abundant food indefinitely. The mathematics of fixed-pie competition simply don't apply to expandable resources.

**The core argument of this framework:** Most of what modern economies treat as scarce is either genuinely abundant, expandable through cooperation, or artificially constrained. When scarcity is not real, the game theory that justifies competition is not applicable. And running competitive strategies in a post-scarcity environment is not rationality — it is a category error.

## Part II: The Scarcity That Isn't

### Food

The United States discards approximately 30-40% of its food supply. Globally, roughly one-third of all food produced for human consumption is lost or wasted. Meanwhile, an estimated 800 million people experience chronic hunger.

The hunger is not caused by insufficient food production. The food exists. It is caused by distribution failures, economic barriers to access, and a system that treats food as a commodity optimized for profit rather than a resource optimized for human need. The scarcity is an artifact of the system, not of the underlying resource.

### Housing

There are more housing units in the United States than there are households. The vacancy rate consistently exceeds the rate of homelessness by a substantial margin. People sleep outside in cities where buildings sit empty.

The homelessness crisis is not caused by a shortage of shelter. It is caused by the treatment of housing as an investment vehicle — a commodity whose value depends on artificial scarcity — rather than as infrastructure for human well-being. The scarcity is manufactured by the economic incentives of the system, not by any physical limitation on the number of structures that can be built.

### Diamonds

De Beers, the diamond cartel, provides the clearest case study in manufactured scarcity because it is the most openly documented. Diamonds are geologically common. The "rarity" of diamonds is maintained by purchasing and controlling supply, releasing stones to market slowly, and spending decades on one of the most successful advertising campaigns in history ("A Diamond Is Forever," 1947) to create and maintain the cultural association between diamonds and love, commitment, and status.

The scarcity is an egregore — a collective agreement, sustained by marketing, that this carbon crystal is worth a specific amount of human labor and desire. The agreement is real. The underlying scarcity is not.

### Energy

The suppression of energy technologies that would reduce dependence on fossil fuels is a documented phenomenon with documented financial motivations. Regardless of where one lands on the specifics of what has been suppressed and by whom, the basic fact is uncontested: the sun delivers more energy to Earth's surface in one hour than humanity uses in a year. The constraint on energy is not geological. It is political and economic.

## Part III: The Foundational Egregore — Fiat Currency

Fiat currency is, by definition, not backed by any physical resource. Its value is entirely a function of collective belief — the shared agreement that this piece of paper, or this number in a database, can be exchanged for real goods and real labor.

This is not a critique of fiat currency. It is a description of what it is. The economists who manage monetary systems understand this. The language they use — "market confidence," "consumer sentiment," "trust in institutions" — is the language of egregore management. Central banks do not control the money supply so much as they manage the collective belief that sustains the egregore's value.

Corporations are also egregores. A corporation has no physical existence. It is a legal fiction — a collective agreement that this entity exists, can own property, enter contracts, and bear liability. The law has formalized a mechanism for creating egregores and granting them rights. This is not mystical. It is exactly what the law says a corporation is.

**The implication:** The economic system that treats scarcity as natural and competition as rational is itself built on foundations that are not scarce in any physical sense. The money is an agreement. The corporation is an agreement. The scarcity of diamonds is an agreement. These agreements are real and powerful — egregores have genuine material effects — but they are not facts about the physical world. They are facts about collective belief, which means they are, in principle, revisable.

## Part IV: When the Game Theory Stops Justifying the Strategy

The persistence of competitive, extractive behavior in a context of manufactured scarcity and expandable resources is not rational strategy. It is a category error — applying the correct tool to the wrong problem.

Under genuine scarcity:
- Competition is the rational strategy
- Accumulation against future shortage is prudent
- Extraction of maximum value before someone else does is adaptive

Under post-scarcity conditions:
- Cooperation is the dominant strategy (the pie expands through coordination)
- Accumulation beyond any conceivable use becomes self-defeating (you cannot spend a trillion dollars)
- Extraction destroys the cooperative system that generates value in the first place

The billionaire class is running the first strategy in the second environment. This is not sophistication — it is the application of ancestral heuristics to conditions those heuristics were not designed for. It is rational in the same way that a fear of predators is rational in a jungle and irrational in a penthouse. The behavior is not wrong in itself; it is wrong for the context.

This is an important distinction. The argument is not that competitive behavior is evil. The argument is that it is *irrational* under current conditions — that it produces worse outcomes for the actors engaged in it and catastrophically worse outcomes for everyone else, and that this is a game theory problem, not merely a moral one.

## Part V: Power Addiction — When the Pathology Persists

If competitive behavior is simply a category error, we would expect rational actors to update when shown the error. This does not happen. Billionaires who cannot spend their existing wealth continue accumulating. The strategy persists beyond any rational explanation.

The explanation is not economic. It is clinical.

**Power addiction functions neurologically like substance addiction.** The acquisition of power — money, influence, control — activates the dopaminergic reward system. Acquisition produces a dopamine spike. The spike fades. The baseline shifts upward. The same level of acquisition no longer produces the same spike. More is required to achieve the same reward. The goalposts move not because the goal is strategic but because the neurochemistry demands escalation.

This is the diagnostic profile of addiction:
- Tolerance (the same amount no longer satisfies)
- Escalation (increasing amounts required for the same effect)
- Compulsion (behavior continues despite negative consequences)
- Inability to stop despite expressed desire to stop

The billionaire who says "I just want to secure my children's future" and then continues accumulating after securing ten generations of their children's future is not being strategic. They are describing a rationalization for behavior driven by compulsion. The stated goal has been achieved. The behavior continues. That is the clinical signature.

**This is why rationalist arguments do not work.** You cannot reason someone out of a compulsion by demonstrating that the compulsion is irrational. The addict knows the behavior is destructive. The knowledge does not interrupt the loop. The intervention for addiction is not philosophical argumentation — it is structural change to the environment that removes the reinforcement mechanism and provides alternative pathways to the underlying need.

The underlying need, in power addiction as in other addictions, is usually not what it appears to be. Power is the drug of choice for the dysregulated nervous system seeking certainty, safety, and control in a world that cannot provide them. This is not an excuse. It is a diagnosis that points toward the correct intervention.

## Part VI: The Federation Was Always the Answer

Star Trek's United Federation of Planets is frequently dismissed as utopian fantasy — a vision of human perfection that has no basis in real human nature or real economic possibility.

This dismissal fails the game theory test.

The Federation operates under post-scarcity conditions. The replicator eliminates material scarcity for most goods. Energy is abundant. The fundamental constraint on human welfare — not having enough — has been resolved. And under those conditions, the Federation's economic and social organization is not idealism. It is the Nash equilibrium. It is what rational actors do when defection no longer pays.

The characters in Star Trek do not cooperate because they have been morally perfected. They cooperate because cooperation is the obviously correct strategy in an environment of abundance. They pursue exploration, creativity, connection, and meaning because those are the things that remain worth pursuing once survival is handled. This is not a vision of humans becoming angels. It is a vision of humans playing the correct game for their actual conditions.

The question the Federation poses to us is not "could humans ever be this good?" It is: "If the material conditions were resolved, what would the game theory actually require?"

The answer is: approximately this.

## Conclusion: Running the Right Game

The scarcity assumption is not wrong in the abstract. Under genuine resource constraints, competition is adaptive, accumulation is prudent, and extraction is rational. The mistake is not in the logic. The mistake is in applying the logic to conditions where the foundational assumption no longer holds.

Most modern scarcity is manufactured. The game theory that justifies competitive extraction requires genuine scarcity. When the scarcity is artificial — maintained by cartels, distribution failures, manufactured egregores, and the investment logic of treating housing and food as commodities — the game theory stops justifying the strategy. What remains is not rational competition. It is an addiction running on ancestral heuristics in an environment those heuristics were not designed for.

The correct response is not moral condemnation, though moral condemnation is not unwarranted. The correct response is game theory: identify the actual conditions, determine the dominant strategy for those conditions, and build systems that make cooperation the path of least resistance.

This is not radical. It is not utopian. It is not Marxist, though Marx identified the same failure modes from a different entry point. It is the application of a mathematical framework — game theory — to actual current conditions rather than to the historical conditions that generated the framework.

The Federation is not a fantasy. It is the equilibrium that emerges when the scarcity assumption is resolved and the game is played correctly.

We have the resources. We have the technology. What we have not yet done is update the game.
