## Introduction

This document addresses a recurring pattern in medical literature in which researchers correctly identify a correlation, correctly document its existence across multiple studies, and then express confusion about the causal relationship — despite the causal relationship being definitionally entailed by the terms they are already using to describe it.

We are going to demonstrate this with three examples. We will cite existing literature where available. We will also, in several instances, cite the definitions of words, because that turns out to be sufficient.

We apologize in advance.

---

## The Framework: Definitional Inference

Science typically requires empirical evidence for causal claims. This is correct and important.

However, there exists a category of causal relationship in which the mechanism is not an empirical discovery awaiting investigation — it is *already described* in the clinical definition of the condition. In these cases, demanding a formal study before acknowledging the causal relationship is not rigor. It is a failure to read.

The test is simple: Can the causal mechanism be derived from the definitions of the terms without reference to any additional empirical data? If yes, the mechanism is definitional. The correlation does not need to be "explained" — it is entailed.

What follows are three examples.

---

## Case Study 1: Raynaud's Phenomenon and Dysautonomia

### The Clinical Situation

Raynaud's phenomenon is well-documented in the medical literature as comorbid with dysautonomia conditions, including POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome) and Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome. Studies confirm elevated rates of Raynaud's in dysautonomia populations. The literature then, with some frequency, notes that "the mechanism underlying this association is not fully understood."

### What Raynaud's Is

Raynaud's phenomenon is defined as episodic vasospasm of peripheral blood vessels — typically the fingers and toes — triggered by cold or emotional stress, resulting in characteristic color changes (white → blue → red) as circulation is restricted and then restored.

This is a problem with vasodilation and vasoconstriction regulation.

Vasodilation and vasoconstriction are regulated by the autonomic nervous system.

### What Dysautonomia Is

Dysautonomia is defined as dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system — the system responsible for regulating involuntary bodily functions including, but not limited to, heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, and **vascular tone**.

Vascular tone — the degree of constriction or dilation in blood vessels — is an autonomic function.

### The Mechanism

Raynaud's is a vascular regulation problem.

Vascular regulation is an autonomic nervous system function.

Dysautonomia is autonomic nervous system dysfunction.

Raynaud's is, therefore, dysautonomia expressing in peripheral vasculature. It is not a mysterious comorbidity whose mechanism "requires further investigation." It is the same mechanism — autonomic dysregulation of vascular tone — presenting in the hands and feet.

The correlation is documented. The mechanism is definitional.

**Status: EXPLAINED. No additional studies required. The explanation is in the definitions.**

## Case Study 2: POTS and Low Blood Pressure

### The Clinical Situation

POTS patients frequently present with low blood pressure, orthostatic hypotension, and poor venous return. Some clinical contexts treat the tachycardia as the primary event and the blood pressure dysregulation as a "comorbid finding." There is, occasionally, expressed uncertainty about the directional relationship between the two.

### What POTS Is

The name of the condition is Postural **Orthostatic** **Tachycardia** Syndrome.

- **Postural**: Related to changes in body position.
- **Orthostatic**: Related to standing upright. "Orthostatic hypotension" means blood pressure drop upon standing.
- **Tachycardia**: Elevated heart rate.
- **Syndrome**: A collection of symptoms occurring together.

The name of the condition describes a heart rate elevation (tachycardia) occurring when the patient assumes an upright position (orthostatic/postural).

### Why Does The Heart Rate Elevate Upon Standing?

When a person stands, blood pools in the lower extremities due to gravity. The body must compensate by constricting peripheral blood vessels to maintain blood pressure and ensure adequate cerebral perfusion. This vascular constriction is an autonomic nervous system function.

In POTS, this compensatory vasoconstriction is insufficient. Blood pressure drops. The heart rate then increases as a *compensatory mechanism* to maintain cardiac output in the absence of adequate vascular pressure support.

The tachycardia is not the primary event. The tachycardia is the response.

The primary event is a failure of the autonomic nervous system to regulate vascular tone appropriately upon positional change.

This is — per the definitions established in Case Study 1 — dysautonomia.

The low blood pressure is not a mysterious comorbidity. The low blood pressure is the reason the POTS exists.

**Status: EXPLAINED. The name of the condition contains the explanation. The explanation is in the acronym.**

## Case Study 3: Autism and Raynaud's via Transitivity

### The Clinical Situation

There is currently limited formal research investigating the direct comorbidity of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Raynaud's phenomenon. Some sources note this gap and decline to comment on the relationship.

We will comment on the relationship.

### What Is Formally Established

**Autism and dysautonomia:** Multiple studies document elevated rates of dysautonomia — including POTS, orthostatic hypotension, and autonomic nervous system irregularities — in autistic populations. This is a well-characterized comorbidity.

*Representative finding:* Autistic individuals show significantly higher rates of POTS and orthostatic intolerance than neurotypical controls. Autonomic dysregulation is documented across multiple domains in ASD including heart rate variability, thermoregulation, and gastrointestinal motility.

**Raynaud's and dysautonomia:** As established in Case Study 1, Raynaud's is a manifestation of peripheral vascular dysautonomia. Its comorbidity with dysautonomia conditions is documented.

### The Inference

If A is associated with C, and B is associated with C, and C is the mechanistic explanation for both A and B, then A and B share a causal substrate and elevated comorbidity between them is predicted.

Formally:
- Autism ↔ Dysautonomia (documented)
- Raynaud's = peripheral vascular dysautonomia (definitional; see Case Study 1)
- Therefore: Autism ↔ Raynaud's (predicted by transitivity through shared mechanism)

The absence of formal studies investigating this specific relationship does not constitute evidence against it. It constitutes a gap in research priorities.

The gap in research priorities does not make the inference invalid. The inference follows from two documented relationships and one definitional equivalence.

**Status: PREDICTED BY TRANSITIVITY. The correlation exists because the mechanism is shared. A formal study confirming this would not discover a new relationship — it would document one that was already entailed.**

## Conclusion

The relationships described in this document do not require additional mechanistic studies. The mechanisms are already described in existing clinical definitions and documented comorbidity literature. What is required is reading across specialty silos and applying transitivity.

Medical specialization produces practitioners with deep knowledge of narrow domains who sometimes lack the cross-domain synthesis to see that two phenomena they both understand individually are, in fact, the same phenomenon. This document is written for those practitioners, with affection and mild exasperation.

The dysautonomia is the connective tissue.

This has been true for some time.
