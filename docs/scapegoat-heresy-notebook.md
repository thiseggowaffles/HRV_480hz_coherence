## Introduction

This document presents a theological critique of Christian nationalism that operates entirely from within Christian theological premises. It does not argue from secular ethics, progressive politics, or external moral frameworks. It argues that the practices central to Christian nationalism — the creation and sacrifice of social scapegoats — constitute a direct heresy against the foundational doctrine of Christian atonement theology, as understood by the very communities practicing it.

This is not a critique from outside the tradition. It is a diagnostic tool built from the tradition's own stated beliefs.

## Part One: The Theological Framework — The Sufficient and Final Sacrifice

### The Scapegoat in the Hebrew Tradition

The scapegoat ritual originates in Leviticus 16. On the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), the high priest would lay hands on a goat, confessing over it the sins of the entire community. The goat then bore those sins symbolically into the wilderness, expelled from the community. The community was thereby "purified." A second goat was sacrificed. Together, they performed atonement.

The mechanism is clear: the community's collective guilt, tension, and sin is transferred to an outsider — a being that cannot consent, cannot defend itself, and is then expelled or destroyed. Order is restored through this expulsion. The community coheres around the shared act of sacrifice.

This is the foundational scapegoat structure.

### Yeshua as the Final Scapegoat

Christian theology — particularly in its evangelical and fundamentalist forms — holds that Yeshua of Nazareth fulfilled and permanently ended this sacrificial system. The central claims are:

**John 19:30 — "It is finished."** The Greek word used (*tetelestai*) is an accounting term meaning "paid in full." The debt is settled. The account is closed.

**Hebrews 10:10-14:** "We have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ *once for all*... For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified." The word "once for all" (*ephapax*) appears repeatedly in Hebrews — it is a theological cornerstone. The sacrifice happened once. It was sufficient. It does not repeat.

**Hebrews 9:26:** "He has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself."

**Romans 6:10:** "The death he died he died to sin, *once for all*."

The doctrinal position is unambiguous: Yeshua's sacrifice was *complete*, *sufficient*, and *unrepeatable*. The sacrificial system — including the scapegoat mechanism — was not merely reformed. It was *ended*.

### The Logical Consequence

If the sacrifice was sufficient and complete, then:

1. There is no remaining debt that requires additional sacrifice.
2. There is no sin so great that it exceeds the scope of what was already paid.
3. There is no community that requires the expulsion of a scapegoat to achieve purity or cohesion.
4. The creation of new scapegoats is theologically unnecessary — and, by the internal logic of the doctrine, an implicit claim that the original sacrifice *was not enough*.

**To create a scapegoat is to confess, in action if not in word, that Yeshua's sacrifice was insufficient.**

This is not a minor theological quibble. This is a denial of the central mechanism of salvation as understood by evangelical and fundamentalist Christianity. It is heresy by their own standards.

## Part Two: The Girard Layer — Why Communities Keep Scapegoating Anyway

### René Girard's Mimetic Theory

The French anthropologist and literary theorist René Girard spent his career documenting what he called the *scapegoat mechanism* — the universal human tendency to achieve community cohesion through shared violence against an outsider.

His argument, developed across *Violence and the Sacred* (1972), *The Scapegoat* (1982), and *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (1978):

1. **Mimetic desire:** Humans desire what others desire. This creates rivalry and social tension.
2. **The sacrificial crisis:** As rivalry escalates, communities teeter toward internal violence.
3. **The scapegoat resolution:** The community unifies by directing collective violence toward a single outsider — typically someone already marginalized, already "other." The scapegoat is blamed for the community's tension. Their expulsion or destruction releases the pressure. Order is restored.
4. **The mythology of guilt:** The mechanism only works if the community believes the scapegoat is actually guilty — actually responsible for the crisis. The scapegoat must be perceived as deserving.

Girard noted that this mechanism runs across virtually every ancient religious tradition — human and animal sacrifice, ritual expulsion, pharmakos (the Greek ritual scapegoat). It is the engine of archaic religion.

### What Made the Gospels Unique

Girard's most controversial and significant claim: the Gospels are unique in the history of world literature because they tell the scapegoat story from the *victim's perspective*.

Every prior account — Greek tragedy, ancient myth, founding narratives — tells the story from the community's perspective. The community was right to expel the scapegoat. Order was restored. The mechanism is validated.

The Gospels invert this entirely. Yeshua is innocent. The crowd is wrong. Pilate knows he is innocent and sentences him anyway. The disciples flee. Peter denies him. The mob demands his execution. The community that achieves cohesion through his death is *not vindicated* — it is *exposed*.

**"Forgive them, for they know not what they do."** The scapegoating crowd is not condemned — they are revealed as operating a mechanism they don't consciously understand. The mechanism itself is made visible.

For Girard, this was the Gospel's revolutionary function: the permanent exposure and intended termination of the scapegoat mechanism. Once you can see it, you cannot un-see it. Once the victim's innocence is legible, the mechanism loses its power.

### What Christian Nationalism Did Instead

Christian nationalism re-ran the archaic mechanism. It took the vocabulary and iconography of the religion whose central text was supposed to end scapegoating, and used it to justify new rounds of scapegoating.

The targets shift historically — Jews, Catholics, communists, the LGBTQ+ community, immigrants, trans people — but the mechanism is identical. A marginalized group is identified as responsible for the community's anxiety and social disorder. Their suppression or expulsion is presented as necessary for the community's purity and survival. The community coheres around the shared identification of the enemy.

Girard would say: they received the Gospel's container but not its content. They kept the sacrifice. They missed the exposure.

## Part Three: Holy Ashes — The Scapegoat Applied to Yeshua's Own People

### Who Palestinian Christians Are

Palestinian Christians are not a modern political category. They are the oldest continuous Christian community in the world.

They are Aramaic-speaking. Yeshua spoke Aramaic. His teachings, his prayers (*Abba* — Father; *Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani*), his daily life were in this language. Palestinian Christians have maintained unbroken linguistic and cultural continuity with the community Yeshua taught and lived among.

They trace their faith to the apostolic period — to Peter's sermon at Pentecost, to the Jerusalem church, to communities that existed before Paul's letters, before the Gentile mission, before the Roman conversion. They are the *original* Christians by any historical measure.

They live in Bethlehem (Yeshua's birthplace), Nazareth (where he grew up), Jericho (where he healed blind Bartimaeus), Hebron, Ramallah, and throughout the West Bank — the actual geographic territory of the Gospel narratives.

### The International Court of Justice Ruling

In July 2024, the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion declaring Israeli settlements in the West Bank illegal under international law, and calling for their removal. The settlements have displaced Palestinian families — including Palestinian Christian families — from land their communities have held since the Byzantine period.

This is not contested among international legal bodies. The settlements violate the Fourth Geneva Convention's prohibition on an occupying power transferring its civilian population into occupied territory.

### What Christian Zionists Are Supporting

Christian Zionism — which represents a significant portion of the American evangelical community, and which constitutes a larger political bloc than American Jewish Zionism — actively supports Israeli military and settlement activity in the West Bank and Gaza.

This means: American Christians, claiming to follow Yeshua, are actively supporting the displacement and killing of Aramaic-speaking Christians who maintain the most direct living continuity with Yeshua's own community, in the geographic territory where Yeshua walked, using doctrine Yeshua's own tradition developed.

**The scapegoat mechanism is being applied to Yeshua's own people.**

The communities cheering this do not appear to recognize the irony. But the irony is structurally precise: if any human community was covered by the atonement, it is the apostolic Christians of the West Bank. If any land was sanctified by the life of the one whose sacrifice these communities claim to follow, it is this land.

The doctrine says: it is finished. No more sacrifice. And yet here is a sacrificial logic being applied to the people most directly connected to the one the doctrine is about.

*Irony wears a crooked crown.* — Holy Ashes, Collective Shadow Work

### WWJD — The Question That Answers Itself

Yeshua was born in Bethlehem, which is in the West Bank. His family was from Galilee, which is within modern Israel, but his ministry repeatedly took him into territory that would today fall under occupation or siege. He was brown-skinned, Aramaic-speaking, living under Roman military occupation, subject to collective punishment, surveillance, and the constant threat of state violence against his community.

What Would Yeshua Do?

The question answers itself if you take the historical Yeshua seriously rather than the Westernized iconographic version. He would be Palestinian. He would be in the West Bank. He would be under occupation.

## Part Four: When Yeshua Came Back — The Scapegoat Applied to the Archetype

### Yeshua as Refugee

Matthew 2:13-15 records the flight to Egypt. Warned in a dream that Herod intended to kill the child, Joseph took Mary and the infant Yeshua and fled across the border into Egypt. They were refugees seeking asylum from a government committing mass violence.

This is not metaphor. This is the biographical record the tradition itself presents. The founder of Christianity was, as an infant, an undocumented refugee who crossed an international border to escape a government killing children.

### Yeshua as the Border Crosser

Imagine Yeshua presenting at the southern border of the United States today:

- Brown-skinned Aramaic speaker from a region experiencing military occupation and collective punishment
- No documentation recognizable to Roman — or American — bureaucratic systems
- Fleeing a situation of political violence
- Arriving with family, seeking asylum

Under current policy frameworks advocated by Christian nationalist politicians, he would be detained. His parents might be separated from him. His asylum claim would face a years-long backlog. He might be deported to the territory he fled.

The communities most loudly advocating for these policies claim to follow him.

### The Internal Contradiction

The scapegoat heresy applies here in its sharpest form. Christian nationalism has constructed a theology in which immigrants — particularly brown, non-English-speaking immigrants from Central America and the Middle East — function as the scapegoat. They are blamed for crime (empirically false — immigrants commit crimes at lower rates than native-born citizens), for economic anxiety, for cultural disruption.

The mechanism is textbook Girard: the community's internal anxieties are projected onto the outsider. Their expulsion is necessary for purity. The community coheres around shared hostility toward the other.

But the archetype they are expelling at the border — the brown-skinned, Aramaic-adjacent, Middle Eastern refugee fleeing state violence — is the same archetype they claim to worship.

They would detain Yeshua. And they would call it Christian.

## Part Five: The Internal Critique as Advocacy Methodology

### Why This Argument Works Differently

Most critiques of Christian nationalism operate from outside the tradition: humanist ethics, international law, secular democratic values. These critiques are valid. They are also easily dismissed by communities that have defined themselves in opposition to secular frameworks.

The scapegoat heresy argument cannot be dismissed on those grounds. It operates entirely from within the tradition's own stated doctrines:

- The atonement is real and complete. (Their claim.)
- Therefore, new scapegoats are unnecessary. (Logical consequence of their claim.)
- Therefore, creating new scapegoats implicitly denies the atonement. (Heresy by their own standard.)

You cannot dismiss this as "secular" without abandoning your own theology.

### The West Bank Approach — Judo, Not Argument

When engaging with Christian Zionists specifically, a productive approach bypasses Gaza and October 7 entirely — not because those questions are unimportant, but because they carry prepared deflections. Hamas is the ready counter-argument. Casualty figures are disputed. The conversation bogs down in contested ground.

The West Bank sidesteps all of this:

- The West Bank is governed by the Palestinian Authority (PLO), not Hamas.
- Hamas has no presence in the West Bank. October 7 is irrelevant to West Bank policy.
- The settlements are declared illegal by the ICJ — this is not disputed by international legal consensus.
- The communities being displaced include Palestinian Christians — their coreligionists.
- The geography is the sacred geography of their own faith: Bethlehem, Jericho, Hebron.

The goal is not to win an argument. The goal is to produce genuine cognitive dissonance — to create a moment where the stated belief and the practiced behavior cannot coexist without one of them yielding. Once that dissonance is felt, the door to broader conversation opens without force.

**Minimum intervention. Maximum effect. Use their own momentum against them.**

This is what Yeshua himself did. He rarely argued. He asked questions. He told stories with endings that forced his listeners to locate themselves inside the story and recognize what they found there.

The parable of the Good Samaritan does not argue that Samaritans are human. It asks: who was the neighbor? And then waits for the answer.

## Conclusion: The Lemonade Recipe, Released

This document does not ask Christian nationalists to adopt progressive politics. It asks them to apply their own doctrine consistently.

If the sacrifice was sufficient, stop sacrificing.

If Yeshua's death covered all sin, stop requiring new deaths to manage community anxiety.

If you follow the one who said "whatever you did to the least of these, you did to me," then what you do to the refugees, to the Palestinian Christians in Bethlehem, to the trans teenager in your community — you do to him.

The scapegoat heresy is not a new problem. It is as old as the moment the first community received a liberating idea and immediately tried to use it to justify the very thing it was meant to end.

The tradition has resources to correct this. It has done it before. The question is whether the communities in question want the actual Gospel or just the brand.
