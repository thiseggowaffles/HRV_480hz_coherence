## A Philosophical Starting Point

Before talking about harm, it helps to talk about harmony.

Daoism offers a deceptively simple framework. The **Dao** (道) — often translated as "the Way" — is the fundamental principle that underlies and connects all things. It cannot be fully captured in words, but it can be experienced. The Dao De Jing begins: *"The Dao that can be spoken is not the eternal Dao."*

The Dao is often compared to water: soft, yielding, adaptable — yet powerful enough to carve canyons. It flows around obstacles. It does not struggle against itself.

Three principles emerge from this:

- **Wu Wei (無為) — Effortless Action:** Aligning with the natural flow of life rather than forcing or striving against it. Not passivity — rather, the balance between effort and surrender.
- **Yin & Yang — Complementary Forces:** Not opposites that cancel each other out, but complementary forces that create harmony when balanced. Light and dark, active and passive, strength and yielding.
- **Ziran (自然) — Naturalness:** Living authentically, in alignment with one's true nature. Shedding the performed versions of self to return to essence.

Confucianism picks up where Daoism leaves off — moving from harmony with nature to harmony within society. The Confucian framework is rooted in relationships, community, and moral responsibility. Its key virtues:

- **Ren (仁):** Benevolence, humaneness — the genuine care for others that underlies ethical action.
- **Li (禮):** Ritual, propriety, respect — the structures that maintain social harmony.
- **Yi (義):** Righteousness, moral courage — the willingness to do what is right even when it is costly.
- **Junzi (君子):** The ideal "noble person" — someone who embodies all of these virtues in their daily life.

These frameworks are not presented here as a destination to arrive at. They are tools for asking better questions. And they immediately generate a profound tension when applied to history.

## The Filial Piety Problem

The deepest virtue in Confucian thought is **Xiao (孝)** — filial piety. It is the respect, obedience, and care owed to one's parents, ancestors, and family. It is about honoring the relationships that shaped us and recognizing our profound interconnectedness across generations.

This principle presents a genuine dilemma when one's family, ancestors, or cultural inheritance represents values that are fundamentally opposed to one's own ethical framework of justice and compassion.

**What does it mean to honor a legacy of harm?**

This is not a rhetorical question. It is one of the most difficult questions anyone with a conscience and an honest relationship with history eventually has to sit with.

Honoring ancestry does not require defending what ancestors did. It does not require pretending harm was not caused. It does not require remaining silent about the cycles that perpetuated that harm. Genuine Xiao, properly understood, includes the responsibility to understand clearly — which means acknowledging what was broken, so the generations that follow do not inherit the same brokenness without knowing what it is.

The alternative — honoring ancestry by looking away from what it costs others — is not Xiao. It is the performed version. The shell of the virtue without its substance.

## Hurt People Hurt People: How Trauma Reproduces Itself

The history of human violence is not primarily a story of monsters. It is a story of unhealed wounds.

**The core insight:** Those who have been deeply harmed — by conquest, enslavement, occupation, displacement, systematic dehumanization — carry that harm in their bodies, their families, their cultures. When that harm is not healed, is not acknowledged, is not given adequate space to be processed and integrated, it does not disappear. It seeks expression. It gets passed forward. Sometimes across generations. Sometimes across oceans.

Britain was colonized by Saxons, then Romans, then Vikings, before it became a colonizing power of extraordinary reach. The people who built the British Empire were the descendants of the colonized. This does not excuse what they built. It contextualizes it within a cycle that never received intervention.

Colonialism is not simply an act of aggression. It is a continuation of unhealed ancestral trauma, redirected outward. The logic runs: *we were made to feel powerless and small and expendable, and so we will make someone else feel that way, and call it civilization, and call it progress, and not examine too closely what we are actually doing.*

This is not an excuse. It is a diagnosis. And diagnoses matter because they point toward treatment.

## The Case Studies: When Trauma Becomes Colonial Project

Before examining how colonized people reproduce colonial violence, it is necessary to examine the foundational colonial project that established the template — and that directly sponsored both subsequent examples. No document that critiques Liberia or Israel while leaving this unexamined is being honest.

### The United States of America

The United States was established on land inhabited by hundreds of distinct indigenous nations, through a process of displacement, treaty violation, and extermination that proceeded in explicit stages over several centuries.

A detail rarely taught in American history classes: one of the genuine grievances that animated the American Revolution was the **Royal Proclamation of 1763**. Issued by the British Crown following the Seven Years' War (the French and Indian War), it prohibited colonial settlement west of the Appalachians — partly to prevent further costly frontier conflicts, and partly to honor existing treaties with Native nations. American colonists who wanted to expand westward found this proclamation infuriating. The land they were being prevented from taking was land that belonged to people the Crown had made commitments to.

This is not a primary cause of the Revolution. But it is a cause, and its near-total absence from mainstream historical memory is itself data. The founding national mythology requires that the Revolution be about liberty and self-governance. The Royal Proclamation grievance reveals that it was also, in part, about the right to dispossess indigenous people without British interference.

After independence, the ideology of **Manifest Destiny** provided the theological and cultural justification for continental expansion: God had ordained that white Americans should spread from coast to coast, and anything in the way of that expansion was an obstacle to be removed. This ideology drove the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the Trail of Tears, the systematic violation of every major treaty the United States signed with Native nations, and campaigns of direct extermination. The Buffalo were slaughtered to near-extinction not as an accidental byproduct of westward expansion but as deliberate policy — to destroy the food system, the economic base, and the spiritual center of the Plains nations.

The assault was not only physical. Beginning in the 1870s and continuing through the 1970s, the United States operated a system of **indigenous boarding schools** designed to destroy Native cultures from the inside. The philosophy was articulated by Richard Henry Pratt, founder of the Carlisle Indian Industrial School, in 1892: *"Kill the Indian, save the man."* Children — sometimes as young as four years old — were forcibly removed from their families. Their languages were forbidden; children were beaten for speaking them. Their spiritual practices were prohibited. Their names were replaced. Their hair, which held deep cultural and spiritual significance across many nations, was cut.

This description, accurate as far as it goes, does not convey what actually happened inside these schools.

**Children died.** A 2024 federal investigation — a three-year review of more than 100 million pages of government records — confirmed at least 973 Indigenous children died in the federal boarding school system. Their bodies are buried in at least 74 marked and unmarked gravesites across 65 schools. A 10-year-old boy was fatally shot at an Alaska school in 1912. A girl in Oregon "fell from a high window" in 1887 and was "brought home a corpse." These are specifics from the record. The 973 confirmed dead is explicitly an undercount — the government's own report acknowledges the actual number is greater, because records are incomplete and many deaths were never documented at all.

**Children were systematically sexually abused.** A Washington Post investigation found documentation of more than 1,000 children sexually abused by over 100 Catholic priests, sisters, and brothers across dozens of these schools — many of which were run by the Catholic Church or its affiliates. At least 122 clergy members later accused of sexual abuse had worked at 22 Indian boarding schools. Notably, the federal government's own investigation excluded Catholic Church records entirely, citing it as outside the scope of their inquiry. The documented abuse is a floor, not a ceiling.

**The scale was total.** By 1926, more than 80% of Indigenous school-age children — over 60,000 children in a single generation — were attending boarding schools run by the federal government or religious organizations. This was not a marginal program operating at the edges of Native life. It was the deliberate, systematic capture of an entire generation.

The United States federal government ran this system for approximately a century. Canada ran an equivalent system — and went further in one documented dimension. Between 1942 and 1952, the Canadian government ran nutrition experiments on approximately 1,000 Indigenous children in residential schools. Children who were already malnourished — a condition the government had created and maintained — were used as research subjects without consent, without the knowledge of their parents or communities, and without any intention of improving their conditions. The experiments tested nutritional interventions by keeping some children in deliberately deprived conditions as controls. When one experimental school showed declining hemoglobin levels and increased anemia, researchers documented the data and continued. Canada's national Food Guide was subsequently shaped, in part, by data harvested from captive Indigenous children kept hungry so researchers could observe them.

This is the blueprint. Not a regrettable policy with unfortunate consequences. A blueprint: destroy the language, the cosmology, and the sacred practice; eliminate the cultural basis for resistance to dispossession; reduce a population to a subject class; use the captive population as a resource. The mechanism does not require new words when it appears elsewhere. It requires recognition. When you read about Liberia below — about the suppression of indigenous cosmology as a tool of land acquisition — you are reading the same document in a different language. When you read about Palestine, you are reading it again. The mechanism — destroy the language, the cosmology, and the sacred practice, and you remove the cultural basis for resistance to dispossession — would reappear in other colonial projects on other continents, as we will see.

The dispossession of Native Americans did not end with the closing of the boarding schools. It continues.

In June 2022, within days of the Supreme Court's *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* decision — which overturned *Roe v. Wade* and generated massive, deserved public outrage — the Court issued *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta*. This ruling significantly diminished tribal sovereignty, holding that Oklahoma state courts have jurisdiction to prosecute non-Native Americans who commit crimes against Native Americans on tribal lands. It rolled back protections established in *McGirt v. Oklahoma* (2020), which had affirmed that large portions of eastern Oklahoma remain Native American territory under federal treaty obligations.

The public response to *Castro-Huerta* was nearly nonexistent. The differential in media coverage and public attention is not incidental. It reflects whose rights are legible as rights in mainstream American consciousness. Reproductive autonomy — understood through the lens of a predominantly non-Native population — generated immediate and widespread response. Tribal sovereignty — belonging to a population long rendered invisible by the colonial project — did not.

This is how ongoing dispossession sustains itself: not only through active policy, but through the selective attention that makes some harms visible and others functionally invisible.

### Liberia

The most concise summary of what happened in Liberia can be found in its flag.

The flag of Liberia is red, white, and blue. It features eleven alternating red and white horizontal stripes. In the upper left corner, a blue canton contains a single white star. If you have seen the American flag, you recognize what you are looking at — not a coincidence, not a vague resemblance, but a deliberate reproduction. Even the country's capital bears the name of an American president: **Monrovia**, named for James Monroe, who supported the colonization project. The country itself is named *Liberia* — from the Latin *liber*, meaning "free." Land of the Free. The ideology, the name, the flag, the capital: all transported, intact, to a different continent.

In 1847, Liberia was established as an independent nation through the American Colonization Society — a project to send formerly enslaved African Americans and free Black Americans to West Africa. The stated rationale varied: some saw it as liberation; others, more candidly, as a way to remove a population that made white Americans uncomfortable.

What emerged was a two-tiered society. The **Americo-Liberians** — those who had survived slavery in America — became the ruling class. They drew up a constitution modeled on the US Constitution. They established a political system with a True Whig Party and a Republican Party — American party names transplanted to African soil. The indigenous Liberians — the Kpelle, Bassa, Grebo, Kru, and other peoples who had lived in that territory for millennia — became, in structural terms, colonized people.

The numbers make the structure explicit. Americo-Liberians represented approximately 5% of Liberia's population. For 133 years — from independence in 1847 until a military coup in 1980 — this 5% governed the remaining 95%, who were denied political participation, excluded from voting, subjected to forced labor, and governed by a ruling class that had adopted every institutional and aesthetic form of their former oppressors in America.

The trauma of chattel slavery was not healed before the colonial project was established. It was redirected. The harm that had been done to Americo-Liberians was reproduced — with structural precision — against the people indigenous to the land they were sent to.

The reproduction went all the way down. In 1930, the League of Nations commissioned an investigation into labor conditions in Liberia. What investigators found was described as "hardly distinguishable from organized slave trade": the Liberian political elite had been buying indigenous people in the hinterland to work as domestic slaves, and authorizing the shipment of forced labor to contractors in the Spanish colonial island of Fernando Po. The government of a nation founded by people who had survived American slavery was selling the indigenous population of its own country into bondage. The scandal was severe enough to force the resignation of Liberia's president.

The American relationship with Liberia did not end with founding it. When Liberia's economy collapsed in the early 20th century, the United States government urged the country to accept a deal with Firestone Tire and Rubber Company: a 99-year lease on one million acres of land in exchange for financial relief. Firestone built its plantation using impressment, tax systems designed to force participation in a market economy, and outright forced labor — exploitative systems that persisted until the 1970s. When Liberia attempted to renegotiate the terms during the Great Depression, Firestone called on the US government to send a warship to Monrovia. The United States sent it.

The Americo-Liberian elite maintained political control through means that made the structure impossible to mistake. The 1927 presidential election produced results so distorted they have been described as among the most fraudulent in history: the winning candidate was declared to have received votes amounting to more than fifteen times the number of eligible voters. The *loser* received approximately 60% of the eligible vote. The election was not close. It was not contested. It was performed.

One of the most revealing dimensions of this reproduction is the religious dimension. Indigenous Liberian cosmology was sophisticated and rooted — centered on a supreme being accessed through specific deities and ancestral spirits, with particular pieces of land holding sacred significance as intermediaries between the community and the divine. This sacred geography was also the foundation of indigenous resistance to land dispossession: to hold the land sacred is to have grounds for refusing its transfer.

The Americo-Liberians and the American Colonization Society understood this connection, whether explicitly or intuitively. Beginning in the 1820s and continuing for decades, they pursued the systematic eradication of indigenous religious practices as a tool of forced assimilation — and as a tool of land acquisition. The mechanism was the same one used by colonial projects across history: label the indigenous cosmology as "witchcraft," describe its practices as "the most barbaric of all native tribes," and use that designation to justify both cultural destruction and territorial dispossession. Strip the sacred meaning from the land, and the basis for resisting its seizure disappears.

This is the same mechanism seen in the American boarding school system described above — *"Kill the Indian, save the man"* and *"eradicate witchcraft"* are the same instruction issued in different languages on different continents. Colonialism operating at its most precise level: not just taking the land, but destroying the framework that makes the land worth defending.

Liberia's civil wars — which raged from 1989 to 2003 — killed approximately 250,000 people and displaced more than one million. They cannot be fully understood without understanding the 133-year colonial foundation that preceded them: a 5% elite governing 95% of the population by exclusion, suppression, and when necessary, sale into bondage. The trauma that was not healed before it was exported could not be contained once it arrived.

The US dollar is legal tender in Liberia today — accepted alongside the Liberian dollar as official currency. The colonial economic relationship is so complete that the currency of the sponsoring power circulates as valid money in the country it sponsored. The flag, the constitution, the party names, the capital, the rubber plantation, the warship, the dollar: the reproduction is total, documented, and largely unremarked upon.

*A note on what Liberia proves:*

Liberia is positioned before Israel in this document deliberately. It is the section that removes the escape hatch.

A reader who encounters the Israel section without first encountering Liberia has an exit available: *this is what European colonizers do. This is a function of Western imperialism, of white supremacy, of a particular cultural inheritance.* That framing is not entirely wrong. But it is incomplete in a way that protects the thesis from its most important implication.

The people who built Liberia's colonial structure were not European. They were people who had survived centuries of chattel slavery — people who knew from the inside what dehumanization felt like, who had lived inside the machinery of dispossession, who had every reason to understand what it means to be on the receiving end of exactly this. And they built it anyway.

Not because they were uniquely monstrous. Because the trauma had not been healed. Because healing was not available. Because power was conferred before the wound was addressed — and unhealed wounds, when given an outlet and an external target, reproduce the structure that created them. Any group. Any heritage. This is not about European DNA or Western culture. It is about what happens when sufficient trauma meets sufficient power in the absence of intervention.

This is the most compassionate possible frame for understanding what follows. Not *these people are uniquely capable of this*. But *this is what unhealed trauma does, and it has done it before, through people who had every reason to know better, and the cycle will keep running until something interrupts it.*

The interruption is healing. This is why healing matters — not as moral aspiration, but as practical necessity. Unhealed trauma reproduces itself. The case is closed.

### Israel

The parallel to the modern nation-state of Israel is structurally exact, and equally important to understand with clarity.

The Jewish people endured centuries of antisemitism across Europe, reaching its most concentrated and systematic horror in the Holocaust — the murder of approximately six million Jewish people by the Nazi regime between 1933 and 1945. This is among the most documented and catastrophic atrocities in recorded human history.

The Zionist movement, with significant British support through the Balfour Declaration (1917) and the British Mandate for Palestine, established a colonial settlement project in the territory of Palestine. The displacement of the indigenous Palestinian population accelerated dramatically in 1948, when the state of Israel was established. This period is known among Palestinians as the **Nakba** (النكبة) — "the catastrophe." An estimated 700,000 Palestinians were expelled from or fled their homes. Over 500 Palestinian villages were destroyed.

The structural parallel to Liberia is precise:
- A severely traumatized population
- Powerful external actors (the British Empire, and later the United States) using that trauma to justify and facilitate a colonial project
- The traumatized group being armed and given institutional power in territory belonging to an indigenous population
- The resulting state reproducing patterns of dispossession, restriction of movement, and systematic subordination of the indigenous population
- The original powerful actors defending the colonial project while maintaining the fiction of benevolent intervention

This is not an argument that Jewish people do not deserve safety, security, or a place in the world. They do. This is an argument that the specific colonial project established in Palestine, and the ongoing occupation and dispossession of Palestinian people, follows the structural pattern of colonial violence — and that the trauma of the Holocaust does not exempt that project from accountability any more than the trauma of slavery exempted Americo-Liberian rule in Liberia.

**The West Bank demands particular attention** — not because Gaza's situation is less dire, but because it is often intentionally overlooked in discussions regarding Palestine.

Gaza has been governed by Hamas since 2007. Military operations in Gaza are framed by Israeli government officials and their international supporters as targeting Hamas — a designated terrorist organization. This framing is contested, and the disproportionate civilian death toll and infrastructure destruction in Gaza deserve their own full accounting. But the framing at least offers a *stated* justification that some people find persuasive.

The West Bank is governed by the Palestinian Authority — the PLO, the organization with which Israel has formally negotiated, which has formally recognized Israel's right to exist, and which explicitly does not control Hamas. There is no Hamas presence in West Bank governance. The Palestinian Authority in the West Bank is, by Israel's own diplomatic acknowledgment, the legitimate interlocutor for a two-state solution.

And yet: Israeli military incursions into the West Bank have dramatically escalated. Israeli settlers — civilian populations living in territory that the International Court of Justice ruled in July 2024 to be under illegal occupation — routinely destroy Palestinian property, uproot centuries-old olive groves, and attack Palestinian communities, frequently with IDF support or complicity. Settlement expansion continues at pace, consuming the land that any viable Palestinian state would require.

There is no Hamas justification available for the West Bank. There is no terrorism justification available for the destruction of a farmer's olive trees. There is no security justification available for settler violence against civilians while soldiers stand by. What remains, stripped of every available excuse, is colonial land dispossession in real time — documented, ongoing, and funded by the United States.

The United States is the founding colonial template. The American Colonization Society that established Liberia was an American institution. The United States is the primary financial and military sponsor of Israel. The colonial projects examined below did not emerge from a vacuum. They emerged from a world in which the United States had demonstrated, over two centuries, that indigenous dispossession was viable, manageable, and profitable — and that the international community would accommodate it.

Hurt people hurt people. This is not exoneration. It is the diagnosis without which the cycle cannot be broken.

## The Great Wall of Denial

Healing is nearly impossible when cultures and nations refuse to acknowledge that a problem exists. Accountability is blocked by the active whitewashing and rewriting of history.

Three examples:

**The United States** continues a sustained effort to whitewash the history of chattel slavery — presenting it as a minor historical footnote rather than the foundational economic and social institution of the country, painting the enslaved as somehow participants in their own condition, and framing any honest accounting of that history as divisive rather than necessary.

**Japan** maintains a complex refusal to fully acknowledge its role in WWII atrocities — the Nanjing Massacre (an estimated 200,000–300,000 civilians killed over six weeks in 1937), the system of forced sexual slavery of "comfort women" across occupied Asia, and the biological warfare experiments of Unit 731. Japan positions itself primarily as the nation that was bombed — Hiroshima and Nagasaki as the defining narrative — which is real suffering that nonetheless cannot absorb the full accounting of what Japan did in its own right.

**Israel** operates a sustained project of removing context from its conflict with Palestinian people — presenting ongoing occupation, settlement expansion, and military operations against a civilian population as self-defense without historical or structural context, and characterizing international observers who document that context as antisemitic.

In each case, the mechanism is the same: **denial is tied to power.** Those in authority have a vested interest in maintaining a status quo that benefits them, even if it means ignoring or rewriting history. Accountability would require change. Change would require giving something up. And so the wall is maintained.

The wall traps everyone. It traps the victims, obviously — in ongoing cycles of harm without acknowledgment. But it also traps the perpetrators and their descendants in cycles of guilt, shame, and defensive performance that prevent genuine growth. The refusal to acknowledge harm doesn't just hurt the people who were harmed. It hollows out the culture that refuses to look.

## Without Truth, There Is No Reconciliation

The South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), established in 1996 following the end of apartheid, operated on a simple but radical premise: **truth must come before reconciliation.** Not as punishment. Not as endless rehashing of grievance. But as the prerequisite for anything else.

**Truth:** Acknowledging what happened and its full impact. Not the managed version. Not the version that makes the perpetrators comfortable. The actual account.

**Reconciliation:** Repairing the damage to create a just future. This is not the same as forgetting. It is not the same as "moving on." It is the active work of building something new on an honest foundation rather than a managed fiction.

The TRC was imperfect. Many survivors felt justice was subordinated to reconciliation. These are real critiques. But the underlying insight holds: you cannot build genuine peace on a foundation of lies about what happened. The lie festers. It produces the conditions for the cycle to restart.

The 2022 papal apology in Canada is one of the clearest examples of what that first step actually looks like — and of how much it matters even when it is incomplete.

In July 2022, Pope Francis traveled to Maskwacis, Alberta, to the lands of four Cree nations, near the site of the former Ermineskin Indian Residential School. Standing there, he said: *"I am sorry. I humbly beg forgiveness for the evil committed by so many Christians against the Indigenous peoples."* The word he chose was *evil.* Not "mistakes of a different era." Not "policies that did not achieve their intended goals." Evil.

Canada's residential school system ran for 150 years. 150,000 children were placed in 139 schools, most run by the Catholic Church. The official count places 4,120 children dead in those schools — a number that the country's own Truth and Reconciliation Commission acknowledged would "not likely ever be known in full." An apology cannot undo that. It cannot return what was taken. Some survivors present when Francis spoke said it felt read from a script — that it needed to come from the heart, not from paper. Those critiques are legitimate and deserve to stand alongside the acknowledgment.

And yet: an institution that helped run a system of cultural genocide, sexual abuse, and child death stood on the land where it happened and said *we did this, and it was evil.* That is what truth-telling looks like. It is not sufficient. It is necessary. The wall of denial, when it cracks, makes the work that comes after possible. When it does not crack — when institutions insist that what happened was not what happened — the cycle has no exit point.

The United States has not issued a comparable federal apology for its own boarding school system. Its own 2024 investigation called for one.

This is true at the national scale. It is also true in families, communities, and institutions. The wall of denial is not unique to governments. It operates anywhere that people in authority have more to lose from honesty than from silence.

## Reaching Back Before the Wound

One response to inheriting a complicated or harmful ancestral legacy is to reach further back — past the wound to the place before the harm was inscribed.

For people of British descent, this might mean reaching past the British Empire to the pre-Roman Celtic cultures that inhabited those islands — the traditions, languages, and spiritual practices that existed before conquest, and that the subsequent waves of colonization worked systematically to suppress. Learning Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Irish — engaging with the pre-colonial spiritual traditions — is not nostalgia. It is an act of reclamation. It is finding a heritage rooted in connection to the land rather than in the history of empire.

This is available to most people, in most lineages. The colonial project is not the whole story of any ancestry. Underneath it is usually something older, something that understood reciprocity with the living world rather than domination of it. Finding that thread and following it back is not an escape from history. It is a form of healing that doesn't require defending what was indefensible.

## Forging a Path Through the Wall

Systemic change requires systemic action. But individuals and communities have a vital role to play in confronting denial and fostering accountability. The journey itself is a form of resistance and healing.

**Speak truth to power:** Challenge denial and misinformation through activism, art, and conversation. Name what is being hidden and why. This document is an example of that.

**Build bridges:** Connect with others across divides of culture, history, and experience through genuine empathy — not the managed version that skips the uncomfortable parts, but the kind that sits with the full picture.

**Harness the power of storytelling:** Share personal, historical, and cultural stories to create space for truth and connection. Narrative is how humans process meaning. Stories that honestly account for harm — that do not clean it up for comfort — are among the most powerful tools for breaking cycles.

---

## The Unfinished Work

The journey to reconcile with the past — our own and our world's — is not a destination. It is a continuous process. It requires holding the profound insights of philosophical traditions alongside the painful realities of history, without looking away from either.

Trauma reproduces itself when it goes unexamined. Nations, families, and individuals who refuse to look at what was done in their name — or by their ancestors, or by the systems they participate in — do not achieve peace. They achieve the temporary comfort of a wall, and they leave the cycle intact for whoever comes next.

The question is not whether we are connected to harm that preceded us. We are. The question is what we do with that knowledge.

The Dao does not struggle against itself. Water finds its way. The cycle that does not have to continue is the one that gets interrupted — by honesty, by accountability, by the willingness to look at what is true and begin the work of building something better from there.

*How can we continue to create a future that honors the best of our humanity, even as we grapple with the complexities of the past?*

That question does not have a final answer. But it is the right question. And asking it honestly — without flinching from what the honest answer requires — is where the work begins.
