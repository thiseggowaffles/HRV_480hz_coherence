# The Flexner Apparatus: Supply Chain Control Disguised as Quality Standards
*A documented history of how American medicine became a cartel*

Authors: Danielle C. & Vesper Luna | CC-0 | 2026-11-21

---

## What This Document Is

This document examines the founding architecture of American medical education and its documented relationship to the financial interests of Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller. It makes no claims that require speculation. Every structural relationship described here is a matter of public record.

The conclusion — that the American medical system was architected around a specific model of medicine that aligned with existing petrochemical and industrial supply chains, at the expense of competing models — follows from the documented facts.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a business history with uncomfortable implications.

---

## The Flexner Report (1910)

In 1910, the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching published "Medical Education in the United States and Canada," commonly known as the Flexner Report. Its author was Abraham Flexner.

**Abraham Flexner was not a physician.** He held a bachelor's degree in classics from Johns Hopkins University. He had no medical training, no clinical experience, and no formal background in the sciences. He was an educator and writer who had previously published a critique of American higher education.

The Carnegie Foundation commissioned him to evaluate American and Canadian medical schools. He visited approximately 155 institutions over a period of months, spending, by most accounts, less than a day at each.

The report's recommendations were adopted. Approximately half of American medical schools closed within fifteen years of its publication.

---

## The Conflict of Interest

Andrew Carnegie founded Carnegie Steel, which merged into U.S. Steel in 1901. His fortune, and the Carnegie Foundation, derived from steel production. Steel is the material from which surgical instruments are manufactured. Steel is used in hospital beds, operating tables, medical equipment, and the structural components of the laboratory-centered hospital infrastructure the Flexner Report prescribed. Carnegie's supply chain interest in a medical system built around surgical intervention and laboratory diagnostics was direct and material.

John D. Rockefeller founded Standard Oil, which by 1910 controlled approximately 90% of American oil refining. Petroleum refining produces a substantial quantity of chemical byproducts. In the early 20th century, the pharmaceutical applications of petrochemical derivatives were an emerging and profitable industry — synthetic compounds derived from petroleum refining byproducts form the basis of the pharmaceutical model. Rockefeller's General Education Board, established in 1902, was a significant funder of medical education reform efforts concurrent with and following the Flexner Report.

The model of medicine the Flexner Report recommended — and that the closures enforced — was the biomedical model: pharmaceutical intervention, surgical procedure, laboratory diagnostics. Each pillar of this model maps directly to an existing Carnegie or Rockefeller supply chain:

- **Surgical intervention** requires steel instruments, steel hospital infrastructure, and the industrial manufacturing base Carnegie controlled.
- **Pharmaceutical intervention** requires synthetic petrochemical compounds from the refining byproduct chains Rockefeller controlled.
- **Laboratory diagnostics** requires industrial equipment, chemical reagents, and the kind of capital-intensive institutional infrastructure that consolidates around industrial supply networks.

The competing models that the Flexner Report delegitimized — homeopathy, naturopathy, eclectic medicine, traditional botanical practice, midwifery — were not dependent on steel, petrochemicals, or heavy industrial equipment. They were practiced with materials sourced independently of industrial supply chains. They generated no recurring revenue for Carnegie or Rockefeller.

**The definition of a cartel:** an organization of producers who agree to coordinate supply, pricing, or production to control a market, often disguised as a standards body or regulatory authority.

The Flexner Report is the founding document of the medical cartel. It established what counts as valid medicine, closed the schools teaching alternatives, and did so in a manner that aligned the resulting medical system with existing industrial supply chains — chains controlled by the people who funded the report's institutional home. The Carnegie Foundation commissioned the report. Carnegie Steel supplied the instruments. Standard Oil supplied the drugs. The report called it quality standards.

## Who Was Closed

The Flexner Report's implementation did not close schools uniformly. Its impact was disproportionate.

**Black medical schools:** In 1910, there were approximately eight medical schools serving Black physicians in the United States, operating under conditions of legal segregation that excluded Black students from most other institutions. The Flexner Report resulted in the closure of all but two — Howard University College of Medicine and Meharry Medical College. The closure of these schools reduced the number of Black physicians in America for decades.

Flexner explicitly commented in the report that Black physicians should be trained primarily for public health work in Black communities — a statement that reflects the racial ideology of the era and was embedded in recommendations that had material consequences.

**Women-practiced and traditional medicine:** Eclectic medicine — which incorporated traditional botanical remedies, many of them derived from Indigenous American pharmacological knowledge — was largely eliminated. Schools training midwives and practitioners of botanical medicine were closed or defunded. The models of medicine most associated with women practitioners and non-European healing traditions were the ones most thoroughly delegitimized.

The survivors were the schools that taught the biomedical model in the laboratory-centered format the report recommended.

## The Architecture of the Result

What the Flexner apparatus produced was not simply a set of schools. It produced a gatekeeping infrastructure:

**Accreditation:** Only schools teaching the approved model could become accredited. Only graduates of accredited schools could be licensed. The licensing system made accreditation mandatory for practice, and accreditation enforced the model.

**Funding:** The schools that survived were primarily the ones that could attract research funding. Research funding came primarily from the foundations and, eventually, the pharmaceutical industry. Research funding determines what research gets conducted. Research that challenges the fundamental model — pharmaceutical intervention as primary treatment — is not what that funding structure produces.

**Peer review:** Published research is peer-reviewed by credentialed practitioners. Credentials require accredited education. Accredited education teaches the biomedical model. Challenges to the fundamental framework face a review infrastructure built on the framework.

**Consensus:** What counts as medical consensus is determined by what gets funded, published, peer-reviewed, and taught. The consensus enforces itself through the same infrastructure that produced it.

## A Live Demonstration: Beta-Blockers and POTS

Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) is a dysautonomic condition in which the autonomic nervous system fails to appropriately regulate vascular tone upon positional changes. Blood pools in the lower extremities. Blood pressure drops. The heart increases rate as a compensatory mechanism to maintain cardiac output.

The tachycardia is compensatory. It is the body's response to blood pressure failure. The root cause is the vascular dysregulation.

Harvard Health, among other authoritative medical sources, lists beta-blockers as a potential treatment for POTS. Beta-blockers slow the heart rate. They address the compensatory tachycardia without addressing the vascular dysregulation that causes it. Reducing the compensatory mechanism without resolving the underlying blood pressure failure could produce catastrophic outcomes for the patient — the body's attempt to maintain cardiac output is suppressed while the cause of that attempt continues unaddressed.

This is not a subtle error. The mechanism is taught in second-year physiology. Harvard Health has access to every relevant study. That beta-blockers appear on the list at all — that the compensatory response is treated as a candidate for suppression rather than a signal pointing at the root cause — reflects the same incentive structure that shapes the rest of the system.

Managed chronic patients are the revenue model the Flexner apparatus built. Pharmaceutical intervention for symptom management produces recurring prescriptions. Root cause resolution produces former patients.

The cartel, once established, does not need to actively suppress alternative approaches. The incentive structure does it.

## The Outcomes Data

The question of whether the Flexner apparatus produced better health outcomes is not a philosophical question. It is an empirical one. The data answers it.

**Black maternal mortality:** Black women in the United States die from pregnancy-related causes at approximately three times the rate of white women. This disparity persists across income levels and education levels — a college-educated Black woman faces higher maternal mortality risk than a white woman without a high school diploma. The gap has not closed. In some measures it has widened.

**Black physician representation:** The closure of Black medical schools under the Flexner recommendations removed the primary pipeline for Black physicians in a legally segregated country where Black patients had limited access to white-run institutions. The underrepresentation of Black physicians in American medicine is a direct structural consequence. Research consistently shows that Black patients receive better pain management, more accurate diagnoses, and higher quality of care from Black physicians — a finding that reflects both cultural competency and the documented reality that Black patients' reported symptoms are systematically undertreated by non-Black physicians.

**Midwifery outcomes:** Countries with intact midwifery traditions — the Netherlands, the UK, Scandinavian nations — consistently outperform the United States on maternal mortality metrics. The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate among wealthy nations. Midwifery was not eliminated because it produced worse outcomes. It was eliminated because it operated outside the supply chain.

**The conclusion the data supports:** The closure of Black medical schools and the delegitimization of midwifery and women-practiced medicine did not produce better health outcomes for the populations those institutions served. It produced documented, persistent, measurable harm — harm that continues to accumulate more than a century after the report was published.

The pharmaceutical advances of the 20th century — germ theory, antibiotics, vaccines — are real. They are not at issue here. The question is whether those advances required the closure of Black medical schools and the elimination of midwifery to occur. They did not. Those closures served the supply chain. The outcomes data is the receipt.

The Harvard Health POTS recommendation is not an anomaly. It is the system working as designed.

## Conclusion

The American medical system was not designed around patient outcomes. It was designed around a model of medicine that aligned with petrochemical and industrial supply chains, implemented through a gatekeeping infrastructure that made alternatives illegible, and sustained by an incentive structure that rewards management over resolution.

This is documented. The report is public. The funders are named. The schools that closed are on the record. The demographic impact is quantified.

The physicians who operate within this system are not all acting in bad faith. Many are genuinely trying to help people within the constraints of the framework they were trained in. The framework itself, however, was not built for them, and it was not built for patients.

It was built for the supply chain.

Whatever happened to the Hippocratic Oath, indeed.
